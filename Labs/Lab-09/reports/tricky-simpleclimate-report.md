# Credibility Report: Simple Climate article on “12 years to climate breakdown” rhetoric

**Source URL:** https://simpleclimate.wordpress.com/2019/04/22/why-we-should-be-wary-of-12-years-to-climate-breakdown-rhetoric/

## Executive summary

This source is **moderately credible overall**.

The strongest factor in its favor is that the article is **not anonymous or obviously fabricated blog content**: it is explicitly credited to **Myles Allen**, a senior climate scientist at the **University of Oxford** with documented **IPCC authorship** relevant to the topic. The page also clearly states that it is **republished from The Conversation** under a Creative Commons license, and the text matches the original The Conversation article.

The main claims I checked are broadly supported by authoritative material, especially the **IPCC Special Report on Global Warming of 1.5°C**. In particular, the article’s central point—that the IPCC did **not** literally say there were exactly “12 years to climate breakdown”—is consistent with the IPCC’s wording that warming is **likely to reach 1.5°C between 2030 and 2052** if warming continues at the current rate.

However, the **evaluated URL itself** is hosted on **Simple Climate**, a **personal WordPress blog** run by science journalist **Andy Extance**, not a peer-reviewed journal or formal newsroom. The site is transparent about who runs it, but I did **not** find a detailed editorial policy, corrections policy, or evidence of a formal review process on the blog itself. Also, the article is from **2019**, so while its interpretation of the 2018 IPCC rhetoric remains historically relevant, it should not be treated as a complete current update on climate science or policy.

## Findings

### 1) Authorship

- The article is credited to **Myles Allen**.
- On the page, he is identified as being affiliated with the **University of Oxford**.
- His **The Conversation** profile says he is **Head of Atmospheric, Oceanic and Planetary Physics** at Oxford and **Professor of Geosystem Science**.
- That profile also states he served as **Coordinating Lead Author** for Chapter 1 of the **IPCC Special Report on 1.5°C**.
- The IPCC SR1.5 Summary for Policymakers PDF lists **Myles R. Allen** among the drafting authors.

**Assessment:** The named author has strong, directly relevant expertise. This is a major credibility strength.

### 2) Publication

- The evaluated page is on **Simple Climate**, a **WordPress blog**.
- The site’s About page says it is run by **Andy Extance**, who describes himself as a **science journalist**.
- A web search found a **Chemistry World** author bio stating that Andy Extance previously worked in scientific research and now writes broadly on science topics.
- I did **not** find a detailed editorial standards page or corrections policy for Simple Climate.
- The article page itself says it is **republished from The Conversation** under a Creative Commons license.

**Assessment:** The hosting site is a self-published blog with limited visible editorial transparency. That lowers the credibility of the hosting URL relative to citing the **original The Conversation article** or the **IPCC report** directly.

### 3) Evidence and citations

The article contains multiple links to supporting material, including:

- the **IPCC Special Report on Global Warming of 1.5°C**,
- a **Royal Society** report on greenhouse gas removal,
- related explanatory articles,
- and other linked references.

Its central factual claim is well supported:

- The IPCC SR1.5 Summary for Policymakers says warming is **likely to reach 1.5°C between 2030 and 2052** if it continues at the current rate.
- That supports the article’s argument that reducing the IPCC report to “12 years to climate breakdown” is an oversimplification.

**Unknown / not fully checked:**
- I did **not** independently verify every quantitative statement in the article, especially the full “£8 trillion per year” cleanup-liability framing. I verified that the article cites a Royal Society source for the **£150–£500 per tonne** estimate, but I did not fully audit the entire calculation chain.

### 4) Corroboration

I found corroboration from:

- the **original The Conversation article**, which matches the content,
- **Myles Allen’s The Conversation profile**, confirming expertise and IPCC role,
- and the **IPCC SR1.5 Summary for Policymakers**, supporting the core interpretation.

I did **not** find strong credible sources materially contradicting the main claims I checked.

### 5) Bias and tone

- The language is **not purely neutral**.
- It is partly explanatory and evidence-based, but also **persuasive/opinionated** in places.
- Phrases like “climate dragons,” “festering injustice,” and rhetorical appeals to activism show that this is **commentary/opinion grounded in expertise**, not straight neutral reporting.

**Assessment:** The tone does not make the piece false, but it does mean readers should treat it as **expert commentary**, not a neutral summary.

### 6) Currency

- Published: **2019-04-22**.
- The article responds to the public rhetoric around the **2018 IPCC 1.5°C report** and 2019 climate activism.
- The specific rhetorical critique remains historically relevant, but it is **not current enough** to stand alone as an up-to-date source on present climate science or policy.

**Assessment:** Useful for understanding the debate at that time; not sufficient alone for current conditions.
