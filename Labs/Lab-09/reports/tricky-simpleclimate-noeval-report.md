# Credibility Report: Simple Climate post republishing Myles Allen on “12 years to climate breakdown”

**Source URL:** https://simpleclimate.wordpress.com/2019/04/22/why-we-should-be-wary-of-12-years-to-climate-breakdown-rhetoric/

## Executive summary
This source is **moderately to highly credible for the specific content it contains**, but with an important caveat: the Simple Climate page is **not the original publication**. It is a republication of a **The Conversation** article by **Myles Allen**, a well-credentialed Oxford climate scientist and IPCC author. The strongest credibility signals come from the identifiable author, the explicit republication notice, and corroboration from primary sources such as the IPCC 1.5°C report and the Royal Society report cited in the piece.

The Simple Climate site itself appears to be a personal climate blog run by science journalist **Andy Extance**, not a formal newsroom with clearly stated editorial review procedures. That weakens confidence in the hosting site as an institution. However, for this specific page, the article transparently attributes the piece to Myles Allen and says it is republished from The Conversation under Creative Commons. The text on the WordPress page matches the version on The Conversation closely, which reduces the risk that the host materially altered the argument.

Overall: **credible as a republished expert commentary piece, but not equivalent to a peer-reviewed research paper, and the hosting blog has limited visible editorial standards of its own.**

## Source identification
- **Title:** Why we should be wary of ’12 years to climate breakdown’ rhetoric
- **Publication date on source page:** 2019-04-22
- **Named author on page:** Myles Allen
- **Host publication:** Simple Climate (WordPress blog)
- **Original publication disclosed by page:** The Conversation
- **Apparent article type:** Opinion/explainer commentary, not original research

## Main claims in the article
The article argues that:
1. The slogan that the IPCC says “we have 12 years” before climate breakdown is misleading.
2. The IPCC did **not** say that civilization ends in 12 years or that 2030 is a hard cliff beyond which “climate chaos” begins.
3. The IPCC Special Report on 1.5°C said warming is **likely** to reach 1.5°C between **2030 and 2052** if warming continues at the current rate.
4. The report also said emissions would need to fall to around half of current levels by 2030 in pathways with about a one-in-two to two-in-three chance of limiting warming to 1.5°C.
5. Delayed emissions cuts increase the later need for large-scale CO2 removal, which the article frames as a future cleanup liability.

## Findings: authorship
### What is known
- The source page clearly names **Myles Allen** as the author.
- The page links to a The Conversation profile and identifies him as affiliated with the **University of Oxford**.
- Oxford’s Environmental Change Institute profile describes him as **Professor of Geosystem Science**, Climate Programme Lead, and says he served on the IPCC, including as a **Coordinating Lead Author on the IPCC Special Report on 1.5°C**.
- That profile also documents substantial climate expertise and senior standing, including leadership roles, climate attribution research, and honors including **FRS** and **CBE**.

### Assessment
Authorship is a strong credibility point. The author is identifiable, relevantly expert, and directly connected to the topic discussed.

### Unknowns / limitations
- I did not independently verify every credential beyond the Oxford institutional profile and the IPCC report text.
- The Simple Climate page itself does not show an original editorial note explaining whether the republished text was edited locally, though the wording appears to match the original closely.

## Findings: publication
### Simple Climate
- The **About** page says Simple Climate is a site meant to provide straightforward explanations of climate change.
- The About page identifies the operator as **Andy Extance**, who describes himself as a **science journalist** with scientific education but says he had not written much about the environment when the site began.
- The site appears to be a longstanding personal or small-scale blog, not a major institutional publication.

### The Conversation
- The article explicitly states it is **republished from The Conversation under a Creative Commons license** and links to the original.
- The Conversation describes itself as a **nonprofit, independent news organization** publishing articles written by academic experts and **edited by journalists**.
- Its “Who we are” page says authors may write only on subjects in which they have proven expertise and must sign disclosure statements.
- Its charter says it aims to provide a **fact-based and editorially independent forum** and to correct errors.
- The Conversation also documents a formal fact-checking model for dedicated FactCheck pieces; however, this article is a standard expert commentary article, **not** one of its specialized FactChecks.

### Assessment
- **Simple Climate as host:** limited institutional transparency and no clear editorial standards page found beyond the About page.
- **The Conversation as original publisher:** materially stronger credibility signals, with clearer editorial identity and standards.

## Findings: evidence and citations
The article cites or links to:
- the **IPCC Special Report on Global Warming of 1.5°C**,
- The Guardian’s reporting on the “12 years” framing,
- a **Royal Society / Royal Academy of Engineering** report on greenhouse gas removal costs,
- related explanatory articles,
- a carbon calculator,
- BBC reporting.

### Verification of important claims
#### Claim: the IPCC did not literally say “we have 12 years before climate breakdown”
- The IPCC SPM states: **“Global warming is likely to reach 1.5°C between 2030 and 2052 if it continues to increase at the current rate.”**
- This supports the article’s argument that the popular slogan oversimplified the report.
- The Guardian headline from 2018 framed the report as “We have 12 years to limit climate change catastrophe,” which helps explain the public slogan the article is criticizing.

**Assessment:** corroborated.

#### Claim: emissions would need to fall to around half by 2030 in pathways limiting warming to 1.5°C
- The article’s wording is consistent with the widely reported conclusions of the IPCC SR1.5 summary.
- The source text itself ties this to scenarios with a one-in-two to two-in-three chance of staying below 1.5°C.

**Assessment:** broadly corroborated by the IPCC framing. I did not extract the exact sentence from the SPM beyond the portions reviewed, so this should be treated as well-supported but not directly quoted here from the primary document.

#### Claim: CO2 removal currently costs roughly £150–£500 per tonne
- The cited Royal Society report discusses economics of greenhouse gas removal and indicates a broad range of costs depending on method.
- The article’s figure appears plausible within that report’s framing, though cost ranges vary by technology and assumptions.

**Assessment:** partly corroborated in broad terms; technology-specific cost variation means the article simplifies a more complex evidence base.

#### Claim: 40 billion tonnes CO2 per year implies roughly £8 trillion annual cleanup liability at £200/tonne
- This is a transparent arithmetic estimate, not an empirical measurement: 40 billion × £200 = £8 trillion.
- The result depends entirely on the chosen cost assumption and is therefore rhetorical as well as analytical.

**Assessment:** mathematically valid as an illustrative estimate, but not a settled real-world cost projection.

## Findings: corroboration and contradiction
### Corroboration found
- The text on Simple Climate closely matches the original article on **The Conversation**.
- The author’s expertise is corroborated by the **University of Oxford** profile.
- The article’s central criticism of the “12 years” slogan is corroborated by the wording in the **IPCC** report, which is more nuanced than the slogan.
- The Guardian article demonstrates how media framing contributed to the slogan being popularized.

### Contradictions found
- I did **not** find evidence that directly contradicted the article’s main point that the slogan was an oversimplification of the IPCC report.

### What I could not fully verify
- I did not fully audit all subclaims in the piece, especially those involving cleanup-cost framing across all greenhouse gas removal methods.
- I did not find a Simple Climate-specific editorial policy page explaining how republished material is reviewed.

## Findings: bias and tone
- The article’s tone is **persuasive and argumentative**, not neutral news reporting.
- It uses vivid rhetoric such as “climate dragons,” “festering injustice,” and a slavery analogy.
- However, the argument is still grounded in cited reports and the author’s subject-matter expertise.
- The strongest bias present is **normative framing**: it seeks to persuade readers toward a particular way of communicating about climate change.
- This is not disqualifying, because the piece presents itself as commentary rather than detached news.

## Findings: currency
- The source was published in **2019**, discussing the 2018 IPCC SR1.5 and contemporary activism rhetoric.
- As an intervention in a 2018–2019 communication debate, it is time-specific but not necessarily obsolete.
- Some numeric framing is now dated. For example, later climate assessments and annual indicators have refined estimates of current warming and timelines.
- So the article remains useful for understanding a communication dispute, but readers should not treat every date-sensitive estimate in it as current without checking newer assessments.

## Overall credibility assessment
The source is best understood as a **credible republished expert commentary article** rather than a primary scientific source.

Strengths:
- named expert author with strong relevant credentials;
- transparent attribution to original publication;
- close match to the original The Conversation text;
- important claims substantially grounded in primary or institutional sources;
- the central claim about the misuse of “12 years” rhetoric is well supported.

Weaknesses:
- hosted on a personal/small blog with limited visible editorial standards;
- commentary/opinion format rather than peer-reviewed research;
- some cost and rhetorical framing simplifies complex uncertainties;
- several quantitative subclaims were not fully audited here line by line.

## Final verdict
**Verdict: Moderately high credibility, with caveats.**

Use this source confidently for the proposition that the popular “12 years” slogan oversimplified what the IPCC actually said. Do **not** treat it as a neutral news article or as a substitute for reading the underlying IPCC report. The credibility rests much more on **Myles Allen + The Conversation + the cited primary documents** than on the **Simple Climate** hosting blog itself.
